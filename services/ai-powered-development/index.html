How it usually goes A composite, not a single client
It starts here
“We should be using AI in engineering.”
VP engineering, B2B SaaS
  1. Week 1We watch four engineers work, a day each. The time is not going into writing code. It is going into working out how the thing they are about to change currently behaves. The bottleneck is comprehension, not typing.
  2. Week 2We instrument the pipeline — cycle time, review latency, rework after merge. Nobody had a number for how long a review waits. It was the largest single block, by a distance.
  3. Weeks 3–5Repository context wired into planning and review. Generated tests aimed at the modules with the worst rework. Review stays exactly where it was, with the author recorded either way.
  4. Week 6A path is found where a suggestion could reach main without review. It is closed the same day. A gate you cannot demonstrate is a gate you do not have.
  5. Weeks 7–8The team runs the workflow without us in the room, and we write down what to do when it produces confident nonsense — because periodically it will.
What we need from you The engagement stalls without these
  • A repository we can readHistory included. Comprehension is the thing being accelerated, and history is where it lives.
  • Two or three engineers who will use it dailyNot a champion who demos it — the people who will hit the annoying parts and say so.
  • Your current numbersCycle time, review latency, rework. If they do not exist we measure them first; there is no other way to know whether it worked.
  • A position on what may be generatedTests, migrations, production code. Someone senior holds that line, and it should be written down before the first suggestion lands.
What usually goes wrong And what we do about it

Adoption is treated as a licence purchase.

We change the workflow, not the tool list. A seat that nobody’s habits accommodate is shelfware within six weeks.

Review gets looser because the author was a machine.

The merge gate does not move. What changes is that a suggestion carries its provenance into review with it.

Speed gets measured in lines.

We measure cycle time and rework after merge. More code, faster, is not the goal and is frequently the failure.

It works for the person who set it up.

Enablement is a phase with a date, not a lunch session. We leave the runbook, the failure cases and the people who have already hit them.

Questions we get asked Before the first call ends
Is this just buying an AI coding assistant for everyone?

No — and if that genuinely is all you need, we will say so on the first call rather than sell you eight weeks. The work here is the repository context, the review path and the measurement around them.

Will our code leave our environment?

That is a decision, not a default. If it cannot leave, the constraint shapes the toolchain from day one — which is a sovereign deployment question and gets answered first.

Does this replace engineers?

It has not in any engagement we have run. It moves where their time goes — less reconstruction of how things work, more judgement about what to change — which is exactly why comprehension is the thing we measure.

What about the code-quality argument?

It is a real argument, and it is the reason the merge gate does not move. We also measure rework after merge, because that is where quality problems actually surface rather than in review.

We build software for our customers, not for ourselves. Does this apply?

Yes — this is that work. Full-stack and SaaS delivery is where the company started; it now runs with agents in the loop. You can hire us to build the product, not only to change how you build it.
