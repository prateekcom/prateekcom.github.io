GenAI implementation

Can we put what we already know to work? Most organisations are sitting on the answer to a question their people look up by hand forty times a day. This is the work of turning that into something dependable enough to put in front of them.

Asked by: the person who owns the process it would change
The shape of it What we hear → what it becomes
What we hear

“Make support smarter.”

What it becomes

A retrieval assistant over your ticket history and product docs, answering with citations, measured on a set of real tickets, and handing to a person the moment it is not sure.

What the work actually is 01 / genai-implementation
  1. 01Ground it in your data — retrieval over the sources that actually hold the answer, rather than a model asked to remember.
  2. 02Make it answerable for: citations on every claim, a confidence threshold, and a defined hand-off to a person below it.
  3. 03Build the evaluation before the interface, from real cases rather than invented ones.
  4. 04Wire it into the tools people already have open, so using it is not a separate decision.
  5. 05Ship behind a flag, watch it on real traffic, then widen.
How it usually goes Illustrative — the shape, not a client account
Where it starts
“Our tier-two agents spend half their day looking things up.”
Head of support, first call
  1. Week 1We sit with four agents and watch. It turns out they are not looking up product facts — they are reconstructing what happened on the account, from three systems. The problem is assembly, not knowledge.
  2. Week 2We pull 300 closed tickets and label what a good answer would have been. Two thirds need account context; a third need the docs. That labelled set becomes the evaluation, and it is written before anything is built.
  3. Weeks 3–6Retrieval across ticket history and the product wiki, with the account timeline assembled first. Every answer carries its sources. Below a confidence threshold it stops and hands over rather than guessing.
  4. Week 7Behind a flag for one team. We watch where it is wrong, not where it is right. The wiki turns out to be the weak link — a third of the bad answers trace to pages nobody has updated since 2023.
  5. Week 8Wiki audit, threshold re-tuned, widened to the rest of the team. The measure agreed at the start — reopened tickets — is the one reported at the end.
What we need from you The engagement stalls without these
  • Access to the real sourcesNot a sanitised export. The mess is the problem we are solving, and a clean sample hides it.
  • Someone who does the work todayA few hours a week from a person who actually performs the task. They will catch what a spec cannot.
  • A decision on the measureWhat number should move, and who agrees it moved. Settled at the start, not argued at the end.
  • A route to productionWhoever owns deployment, in the room early. Capabilities that cannot be shipped are expensive demos.
What usually goes wrong And what we do about it

The demo answers beautifully and production answers badly.

Demos are run on the questions someone thought of. We build the evaluation set from real traffic first, so the hard cases are in scope before the easy ones are impressive.

It is confidently wrong, and nobody can tell.

Citations on every claim, and a confidence threshold below which it declines rather than guesses. A system that says “I don’t know” is worth more than one that is right most of the time.

The underlying content is stale, and the model faithfully repeats it.

Retrieval makes content quality visible for the first time. We report what the sources get wrong — that finding is often worth more than the assistant.

It launches, and usage quietly dies.

It goes where the work already happens rather than behind another login, and we widen from one team only once the numbers hold.

Questions we get asked Before the first call ends
Can this run without our data leaving our environment?

Yes, and it is a design decision made at the start rather than a retrofit. Where residency, retention or key ownership are hard requirements, that constraint shapes the architecture from the first line — see Sovereign AI.

Which model do you use?

Whichever one measures best on your task, and we will show you the comparison rather than assert it. In most retrieval work the model is not the deciding variable — the quality of the sources and the retrieval step matter more. If the choice genuinely is the open question, that is applied research.

How do you stop it making things up?

Ground every answer in retrieved sources, cite them, and set a confidence threshold below which it declines and hands to a person. Then measure how often it is wrong on real cases, publish that number, and treat it as the thing to improve rather than the thing to hide.

What happens when you leave?

You keep the evaluation set, the documentation and a team that has been doing the tuning alongside us for the last few weeks. If we have done this properly, the handover is uneventful.

We tried this last year and it did not stick. Why now?

Usually because it was measured on demos rather than on real work, or because it lived somewhere nobody goes. Both are fixable, and knowing what failed the first time makes the second attempt considerably cheaper. Bring the post-mortem to the first call.
