  2. 02Assess each for value, feasibility and risk — including the ones we will recommend against.

How it usually goes Illustrative — the shape, not a client account
It starts here
“The board wants an AI roadmap.”
COO, manufacturing group
  1. Week 1We interview eleven people across four functions. Nineteen candidate use cases come out of it. Four of them are the same use case, described by four departments who have not spoken to each other.
  2. Week 2Each is assessed for value, feasibility and risk — with the people who own the process, not only the people who own the budget. Two of the most-wanted turn out to need data nobody collects.
  3. Week 3Ranked, with the reasoning written next to each one. The candidate the board expected to win comes fourth, and the page says exactly why.
  4. Week 4One pilot brief: the problem, the measure, the data, the people, and what happens in the first two weeks. Specific enough to start on Monday.
  5. AfterwardsThe discarded options stay legible with their reasons attached, so a change of sponsor does not restart the argument from the beginning.
What we need from you The engagement stalls without these
  • Process owners, not only sponsorsThe people who do the work know which step is genuinely painful. Budget holders know which step is visible. Both are useful; only one is enough.
  • An honest view of the dataIncluding what is not collected. Half the candidates die here, and it is far better that they die in week two.
  • A real constraint on time and money“Unlimited” produces a roadmap nobody funds. Tell us the actual envelope and the ranking becomes useful.
  • Agreement on who decidesA ranking with no owner becomes a document. We need the person who will stand behind the choice in the room.
What usually goes wrong And what we do about it

The roadmap is a list of technologies.

We rank problems and outcomes. The technology is a consequence of the choice rather than the subject of it.

The loudest use case wins.

Every candidate is scored the same way with the reasoning beside it, so disagreement has something specific to argue with.

Feasibility is assessed without looking at the data.

We look. It is the fastest honest way to remove half the list, and it happens before anyone gets attached.

The discarded options vanish.

They stay in the document with their reasons. A change of sponsor should not mean holding the same argument twice.

Questions we get asked Before the first call ends
How long does this take?

Typically four weeks for a mid-sized organisation. Faster is possible and usually means fewer interviews — which is precisely where the value of the exercise sits.

What if the answer is “none of these”?

Then we say so, and four weeks has saved you a year. It has happened, and from where we sit it is a good outcome rather than an embarrassing one.

Do you then build the pilot?

Often, but not necessarily — and the brief is deliberately written so that anyone could. If we were the only firm able to execute it, it would be a bad brief.

We already have a shortlist. Can you just validate it?

Yes, although we will ask what is not on it. The most common finding is that the strongest candidate was never proposed, because it sits between two departments and belongs to neither.

Is this a workshop?

There are sessions in it, but the deliverable is a ranked assessment and a pilot brief — not a set of photographed sticky notes and a summary email.
